Restorative justice is an approach to harm that asks who was affected, what they need, and who has a responsibility to respond. It invites people to consider what accountability and repair could look like in their situation.

Start by listening

A restorative conversation leaves room for people to describe their experiences in their own words. A facilitator can help participants agree on how to share time, listen, and take a break. Participation and disclosure should be voluntary.

Recognize harm and responsibility

People can experience the same event differently. Listening to those perspectives can help clarify what happened and identify concrete needs. Accountability can include acknowledging harm, agreeing to actions, and checking whether those actions are carried out.

Choose practices with care

A circle is one possible way to structure a conversation. Participants should be able to pass or leave, and a process should be planned with safety, consent, accessibility, and power differences in mind.

Consider where it fits

Restorative practices can help a group build relationships before conflict arises or respond to harm after it occurs. The purpose and preparation matter. A community-building circle can use a shared prompt and make space for people to pass. A process responding to harm calls for separate preparation, agreement about what can be shared, and a plan to follow up on commitments.

People affected by harm should be able to decide whether to participate, what support they need, and when to pause. A restorative conversation can sit alongside other ways to seek safety, assistance, or accountability.
